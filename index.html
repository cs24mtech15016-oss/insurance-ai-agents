Domain-Specialized AI Agents for Insurance on an Open-Source Stack

A study of Model Ops and Cost Ops: algorithms, systems, and economics

Author: N. Mandipati. Date: 2026-09-18.

Sources: a market survey plus five literature reviews (model routing, context/KV optimization, PEFT and preference tuning, distillation, serving internals, and insurance-domain NLP/IDP). Papers are cited by arXiv ID. Benchmark numbers are as reported by the cited work and are author-reported unless noted as independently reproduced.

Abstract

Insurance is a good fit for agentic AI: the work is document-heavy, rule-bound, high-volume, and split between routine and complex cases, and every task has a dollar-denominated KPI (loss ratio, combined ratio, cycle time, straight-through-processing rate). This study surveys the 2025–2026 market of insurance AI agents, then designs an open-source stack that reproduces their capabilities, and treats two questions in detail: how to train and deploy domain LLMs and SLMs (Model Ops), and how to route, compress, and measure cost (Cost Ops).

The argument is that a single frontier model is rarely the right answer. A distilled small model behind a router usually wins on cost at equal task quality. Model Ops produces the cheap specialist; Cost Ops decides when it is safe to use and compresses what it reads. The two are linked by one quantity, the escalation-penalized cost per resolved task, so they have to be tuned together. Chapter 6 gives the coupling, a self-host-versus-API break-even, and a distill-then-route cost model. Every component is tied back to the primary literature.

Chapter 1 — Problem Framing

Four properties of insurance work shape the design.

Document density. Every workflow begins with unstructured intake: ACORD forms, loss runs, statements of value, medical bills, police reports, dec pages. Intelligent Document Processing (Chapter 7) is therefore the base capability, not an add-on.

Rule-boundedness. Underwriting guidelines, policy wording, and regulation are explicit rules. This suits RAG grounding and, more importantly, the citable explainability regulators expect.

Volume with a long tail. Roughly 80–90% of claims and submissions are near-identical routine cases; 10–20% are genuinely complex. That distribution is what makes routing worthwhile (Chapter 5).

Dollar-denominated outcomes. Each task maps to a KPI, so the right optimization target is cost per resolved task, not cost per token (Chapter 6).

Taken together these point to one pattern: send the routine majority to a cheap distilled specialist, escalate the hard minority to a frontier model, and measure the system in resolved-task economics.

2.2 Where the open-source gap is

The vendors’ advantage is proprietary insurance data (Bevaya’s 300M documents, Cytora’s pretrained models, Shift’s cross-carrier network) plus explainability and audit tooling. It is not model architecture. Off-the-shelf open-source orchestration and IDP give you the scaffolding but not the domain knowledge.

For a carrier that owns its claims and policy corpus, the open-source path is: assemble the scaffolding (Chapter 3), specialize by distillation and PEFT on that corpus (Chapter 4), run it economically (Chapters 5–6), and keep the data advantage in-house. Vendor ROI figures above are self-reported and directional.

Two names sometimes cited in this space were excluded as unverifiable in current form: “Sixfold” (pivoted to logistics) and “Gradient AI” (ambiguous redirect).

Chapter 3 — Open-Source Reference Architecture

Orchestration, chosen by workflow shape. LangGraph for stateful graphs with checkpoints (long document pipelines). CrewAI for role-based multi-agent flows (claims triage). Pydantic AI for typed, durable execution via Temporal/Prefect/DBOS with OpenTelemetry built in, which suits long human-in-the-loop approvals. Haystack and LlamaIndex are alternatives for integration-heavy and document-centric cases.

Serving. vLLM leads: PagedAttention, continuous batching, chunked prefill, prefix caching, and multi-LoRA/S-LoRA (Chapter 4B). TGI is now in maintenance mode. SGLang is a strong alternative built around RadixAttention. Ollama and llama.cpp cover on-prem private inference.

Models. Reasoning-heavy adjudication: Llama 3.x 70B or Qwen 2.5-14B. Multilingual claims: Qwen 2.5 with Qwen3Guard. OCR and extraction: Mistral-OCR, Donut, LayoutLMv3. Triage and routing SLMs (1–8B): Phi, Gemma 2/3, Qwen 1.5–7B.

RAG. Parsing with Docling, LlamaParse, or Unstructured. Embeddings with BGE (MIT-licensed, MTEB leader) plus bge-reranker. Vector store: Qdrant (Rust, quantization, hybrid search) or Weaviate (multi-tenant, RBAC); pgvector if you are already on Postgres.

Guardrails and observability. Guardrails AI and NeMo Guardrails with Qwen3Guard. Langfuse for traces and cost (ClickHouse backend), Phoenix/Arize for development, Ragas or DeepEval for RAG evaluation, and OpenTelemetry GenAI semantic conventions.

Chapter 4 — Model Ops: Training Domain LLMs and SLMs

4.1 When to adapt, and how

Need new FACTS?              → RAG (citable, auditable, no retraining)
Need STYLE/FORMAT/behavior?  → Fine-tune
  ├─ <1K examples            → prompt + few-shot + RAG
  ├─ 1K–10K, single task     → SFT with LoRA/QLoRA
  ├─ 10K+, preference        → SFT + DPO/ORPO/KTO/SimPO
  └─ domain corpus (M tokens)→ continued pretraining (DAPT) → SFT
Low budget / on-prem?        → QLoRA (4-bit) or distill to an SLM
Production?                  → quantize (GPTQ/AWQ/FP8) + multi-LoRA serving

The short rule: RAG adds facts, fine-tuning changes form and behavior. Reach for fine-tuning when you need consistent format or a specific decision style, not when you need the model to know a new fact.

4.2 Parameter-efficient fine-tuning

LoRA (arXiv:2106.09685) freezes the base weight W₀ and learns a low-rank update:

h = W₀x + (α/r)·BAx,   B∈ℝ^{d×r}, A∈ℝ^{r×k}, r≪min(d,k)

B is zero-initialized and A uses Kaiming init, so the update is identity at the start. The scale is α/r (α typically 16–32). Target the attention projections (q,k,v,o) and MLP (gate,up,down). Trainable parameters are about 2·d·r per layer. On GPT-3 175B the paper reports 10,000× fewer trainable parameters, 3× less GPU memory, on-par quality, and no added latency once merged.

QLoRA (arXiv:2305.14314) back-propagates through a frozen 4-bit base into LoRA adapters. It combines NF4 quantization (near-optimal for normally distributed weights), double quantization (quantizing the quant constants, saving about 0.4 bit/param), and paged optimizers. The result is a 65B fine-tune on a single 48GB GPU, versus roughly 1,200GB in fp16, matching 16-bit quality on Vicuna and MMLU.

Variants worth knowing. DoRA (2402.09353) splits the weight into magnitude and direction and applies LoRA to the direction only; it beats LoRA consistently with no inference overhead. rsLoRA uses α/√r to stabilize high ranks. LoRA+ raises the learning rate on B for roughly 2× faster convergence. PiSSA initializes adapters from SVD principal components. VeRA shares random projections across layers to cut memory.

4.3 Preference optimization

DPO (arXiv:2305.18290) rewrites RLHF as a closed-form classification loss with no reward model and no PPO:

ℒ_DPO = −E_{(x,y⁺,y⁻)} log σ( β[ log(π_θ(y⁺|x)/π_ref(y⁺|x)) − log(π_θ(y⁻|x)/π_ref(y⁻|x)) ] )

The implicit reward is r(x,y)=β·log(π_θ/π_ref), with β in 0.1–0.5. ORPO (2403.07691) is reference-free and folds preference into SFT through an odds-ratio penalty, removing the separate alignment phase; on UltraFeedback it lifts small models (125M–7B) above some 13B baselines. KTO (2402.01306) uses a prospect-theory loss and needs only binary good/bad labels rather than pairs. SimPO (2405.14734) is reference-free with an average-log-prob reward and a target margin, reported to beat DPO by up to 6.4 points on AlpacaEval2. GRPO (DeepSeekMath, 2402.03300) drops the critic and uses group-relative advantage A_i=(r_i−mean(r_G))/std(r_G), cutting memory roughly 2.7× versus PPO.

Method Use when
DPO paired preferences, want simplicity and stability
ORPO want single-stage SFT+align, no reference model
KTO only have binary good/bad labels
SimPO want reference-free and length-controlled
GRPO verifiable-reward tasks (math, code, rules), no critic

4.4 Distillation

Sequence-level KD trains the student on teacher-generated text. MiniLLM (2306.08543) uses reverse KL, KL(π_student‖π_teacher), so the student does not over-cover the teacher’s low-probability mass, and trains on the student’s own generations; across 120M–13B it improves calibration and reduces exposure bias. GKD (2306.13649) is on-policy: the teacher scores the student’s generations, fixing the train/inference mismatch. DistiLLM (2402.03898) adds a skew-KL loss and adaptive off-policy sampling, reported at 4.3× faster than prior KD at comparable quality. For synthetic data, Evol-Instruct/WizardLM (2304.12244) iteratively raises instruction complexity and reached over 90% of ChatGPT on 17 of 29 skills.

A concrete insurance recipe: a teacher (a frontier model or Llama-70B) labels claims decisions and extractions; distill to a 3–7B student with MiniLLM or GKD; align with KTO on adjuster accept/reject labels; quantize to 4-bit; serve on vLLM multi-LoRA with per-carrier adapters. This reproduces a vendor “insurance-trained model” on your own corpus.

4.5 Continued pretraining and forgetting

DAPT (arXiv:2004.10964) adds a second LM-objective pretraining phase on in-domain text before task fine-tuning, with gains in both high- and low-resource settings; task-adaptive pretraining helps further. Guard against catastrophic forgetting with replay (mix in general data), LoRA-based continued pretraining (freeze the base, adapt in adapters), or EWC.

4.7 Data and evaluation

Curate with exact and fuzzy dedup (MinHash-LSH), perplexity or classifier quality filters, and decontamination (remove 13-gram overlaps with eval sets). Evaluate with lm-evaluation-harness (EleutherAI; 60+ benchmarks; vLLM/HF/API backends). LLM-as-judge correlates only about 0.4–0.6 with humans and carries position and length bias, so debias it and keep domain-specific human review. For compliance, keep PII/PHI on-prem or in-VPC, version every checkpoint, adapter, and dataset, log all inferences, prefer RAG for citability, and write a Model Card (arXiv:1810.03993) per artifact.

Chapter 4B — Serving Internals

4B.1 PagedAttention and vLLM (arXiv:2309.06180, SOSP 2023)

Paging the KV cache into fixed-size blocks cuts fragmentation waste from 60–80% to under 4%, enables prefix sharing, and supports continuous batching. Reported throughput is 14–24× over HF Transformers and 2.2–3.5× over TGI; LMSYS ran 5× the traffic on half the GPUs in production.

4B.2 Multi-LoRA serving

This is what makes the multi-domain design in Chapter 8 cheap. S-LoRA (arXiv:2311.03285) unifies paging of adapter weights and KV cache, batches heterogeneous ranks with custom CUDA kernels, and keeps adapters in host memory until needed, serving thousands of adapters at roughly 4× the throughput of HF PEFT or naive vLLM-LoRA. Punica (arXiv:2310.18547) adds the SGMV kernel, which batches different adapters against one shared base copy for 12× throughput at about 2ms per token. Adding a domain adds an adapter, not a GPU.

4B.3 Quantization

Method Mechanism Reported
GPTQ (2210.17323) layer-wise Hessian/OBQ, 3–4 bit 3.25× (A100) / 4.5× (A6000); 175B in ~4 GPU-hr
AWQ (2306.00978) activation-aware per-channel scaling, protect ~1% salient 3×+ vs FP16; 70B at 4-bit on a mobile GPU
SmoothQuant (2211.10438) shift activation outliers to weights, W8A8 1.56× speed, 2× memory; 530B on one node

4B.4 Speculative decoding

Draft-and-verify (Leviathan, 2211.17192) gives 2–3×. Medusa (2401.10774) adds extra heads with tree attention (2.2–3.6×). EAGLE/2/3 (2401.15077) does feature-level autoregression for up to 5.6× on 13B. All are distribution-preserving, so quality is unchanged.

4B.5 Guided decoding

For schema-constrained extraction, use Outlines (FSM-based), xgrammar (2411.15100, up to 100× faster constrained generation with near-zero overhead), or lm-format-enforcer. This is what makes ACORD/FNOL JSON extraction reliable.

4B.6 Orchestration

SGLang RadixAttention gives up to 6.4× over vLLM/Guidance/LMQL on shared-prefix workloads. KServe provides InferenceService and ModelMesh; Ray Serve adds fractional-GPU scheduling and composition; BentoML handles packaging and BYOC. llm-d and NVIDIA Dynamo are emerging options for disaggregated prefill/decode.

4B.7 Serving efficiency: the latency–rate (L/R) frontier

The router L/R in 5.1.1 governs which model runs; the serving L/R governs how efficiently a given model runs on the GPU. Per-request latency has two parts:

Latency = TTFT (prefill, compute-bound) + N_out · TPOT (decode, memory-bandwidth-bound)

The efficiency curve is latency L as a function of request rate R (QPS or concurrency). As R rises, continuous batching raises throughput and utilization U, but per-request L rises too. The two trade off along a curve with a knee; efficient serving means operating near that knee, with U high and L still under the SLO. The right KPI is goodput, requests per second that meet the SLO, not raw throughput.

Why the curve bends: decode has low arithmetic intensity I = FLOPs / bytes_moved, because loading weights and KV per token dominates, so a single stream is memory-bandwidth-bound and wastes compute. Batching amortizes the weight load across requests, raising effective I and U, until KV bandwidth or capacity (and eventually compute) becomes the bound. This is the systems reason PagedAttention and continuous batching (4B.1), GQA/MQA, and KV quantization (5.2) matter. It also connects back to 5.1.1: routing concentrates traffic on the SLM, which means larger batches and higher U, so the router L/R and the serving L/R improve the same cost line at once. Chunked prefill (vLLM) tunes the TTFT-versus-TPOT split directly.

Two SLA classes matter in insurance. Interactive work (agent turns, chat, real-time FNOL triage) is bound by TTFT and TPOT, so keep R below the knee. Batch work (overnight document indexing, bulk loss-run extraction) is throughput-bound, so push R past the knee to maximize U. Serve them on separate queues or replicas, or with priority scheduling, and report L-vs-R and goodput per class.

Chapter 5 — Cost Ops: Routing and Context Optimization

5.1 Model routing

RouteLLM (arXiv:2406.18665) learns a binary router R: query→{strong,weak} and routes to the strong model when P(strong wins|q)>α. It offers four routers:

  • Similarity-weighted Bradley-Terry: non-parametric, cosine-weighted (γ=10) over Arena battles, P(strong wins)=σ(ξ_s−ξ_w), no gradient training, adds under 0.4% cost.
  • Matrix factorization: a bilinear win-probability model on query and model embeddings.
  • BERT classifier: CLS token plus a logistic head, full fine-tune.
  • Causal-LLM classifier: Llama-3-8B predicting the label token.

The metrics are PGR (performance gap recovered), APGR (PGR integrated over cost), and CPT(x%) (minimum strong-model calls for a target PGR). On MT-Bench (GPT-4 vs Mixtral-8x7B), matrix factorization reaches APGR 0.802 and up to 3.66× cost reduction at about 95% of GPT-4 quality; on MMLU it needs about 20% fewer GPT-4 calls at 50% PGR. The routers transfer to other model pairs (Claude, Llama-3.1) without retraining.

FrugalGPT (arXiv:2305.05176) runs an LLM cascade cheapest to dearest, accepting answer a_i when a DistilBERT reliability scorer g(q,a_i)≥τ_i, and solves max E[r] s.t. E[cost]≤b as a mixed-integer program. It reports up to 98.3% cost reduction on HEADLINES at 1.5 points above GPT-4.

Hybrid LLM (arXiv:2404.14618) routes on the quality gap H(x)=Q_small−Q_large with a DeBERTa-v3 router, in deterministic, probabilistic, and transformed modes. It cuts large-model calls by about 22% at a 1% quality drop, adding roughly 0.036s.

AutoMix (arXiv:2310.12963) is a self-verification cascade with a POMDP meta-router ⟨S,A,T,R,Ω,O⟩. It classifies queries as easy, complex, or unsolvable (so it avoids wasteful escalation on the unanswerable), uses reward P−λC, and solves with an AdaOps particle filter in under 1ms. It reports over 50% cost reduction, beating FrugalGPT and Hybrid LLM, and works with about 50 training examples.

For benchmarks: RouterBench (2403.12031; 405,467 samples, 11 models, 8 datasets) defines AIQ (area under the non-decreasing convex hull) and finds that cheap models such as Mistral-7B and WizardLM-13B are often optimal, GPT-4 is seldom chosen, and cost varies 2–5× at equal quality; cascading beats predictive routing when judge error stays under 0.1 and degrades past 0.2. GraphRouter (2410.03834) is a heterogeneous GNN with edge prediction that generalizes to new LLMs (+9.52% over C2MAB-V, 99.45% less onboarding time). RouterDC (2409.19886) uses dual contrastive learning on an 86M mDeBERTa (+3.98% in-distribution, about 6× faster than voting). Aurelio’s semantic-router (MIT) does embedding cosine-similarity routing in about 10ms.

Predictive versus cascade. A predictive router is a single forward pass with low latency, but it is bounded by predictor accuracy and needs calibration to the deployment distribution. A cascade is self-correcting and can beat any single model, but it adds sequential and verification latency and degrades quickly when the assumption that the larger model is at least as good as the smaller one fails (about 6–13% of queries in FrugalGPT). For insurance, a predictive domain-and-difficulty router at the front door keeps latency low for real-time FNOL, with a cascade fallback on low confidence.

5.1.1 The light-to-remote (L/R) ratio

The literature standardized on quality-at-cost curves (APGR, CPT, AIQ), but the day-to-day operational measure of a deployed router is the light-to-remote call ratio: how many queries the cheap or local model absorbs per query that reaches the expensive or remote one. Let the router send fraction p of traffic to the light SLM, which has first-pass success s. Every locally attempted query hits the light model once, and a fraction (1−s) escalate. Over N queries:

light calls   = p·N
remote calls  = (1−p)·N + p·(1−s)·N = N·(1 − p·s)

L/R = light_calls / remote_calls = p / (1 − p·s)

The limits make the coupling clear. When s→1 the SLM is always right and L/R = p/(1−p), pure routing gain. When s→0 the SLM is useless and L/R → p: you pay for the light call and still hit the remote model on everything. That is the escalation penalty of Chapter 6 showing up as a collapsed ratio. The remote-touch rate is 1 − p·s, and blended cost versus an all-remote baseline is:

C_blended / C_remote = (1 − p·s) + (C_light / C_remote)·p

L/R on its own is misleading, which is why RouteLLM and RouterBench did not adopt it as a headline number: a router maximizes it trivially by sending everything to the light model and destroying quality. Read it together with a quality metric. L/R is the operational efficiency KPI (it drives cost and GPU utilization); APGR, CPT, and AIQ are the quality-at-cost gauges that keep it honest. In insurance terms, L/R tells you how much of the routine 80–90% the SLM is actually catching. A falling L/R at fixed PGR is the earliest sign of SLM drift and the trigger for re-distillation (Chapter 6). Note that raising s through Model Ops raises L/R without hurting quality, so both disciplines pull the same lever.

5.2 Context optimization

LLMLingua (arXiv:2310.05736) prunes tokens by perplexity using a small LM. A budget controller sets different rates by segment (instructions around τ=0.85, questions τ=0.9, demonstrations compressed harder), then iterative token-level compression runs over ~100-token segments with per-segment thresholds, with distribution alignment. On GSM8K it reaches 5× compression at 79.08 EM (versus 78.85 uncompressed) and 20× at 77.33, at about 1/25 of the target model’s overhead. LongLLMLingua (2310.06839) makes it question-aware, with document ranking r_k, contrastive perplexity s_i=ppl(x_i|x_<i)−ppl(x_i|x_que,x_<i) (roughly conditional PMI), document reordering to fight “lost in the middle,” and subsequence recovery, for +21.4% at about 4× on NaturalQuestions and 94% cost reduction on LooGLE. LLMLingua-2 (2403.12968) casts it as token classification with an XLM-RoBERTa model distilled from GPT-4 data, 3–6× faster than v1.

Prefix and KV caching. vLLM’s automatic prefix cache reuses blocks by hash. SGLang RadixAttention (2312.07104) keeps KV pages in a radix tree with LRU eviction and longest-shared-prefix scheduling; it reports up to 6.4× throughput and 3.7× lower latency, with 50–99% hit rates in production. In insurance, prefix-cache the stable policy and guideline block once and reuse it across every claim.

KV compression. H2O (2306.14048) evicts all but heavy-hitter tokens (a submodular selection) and reaches up to 29× throughput at 20% cache. StreamingLLM (2309.17453) keeps a few attention-sink tokens plus a rolling window, taking perplexity from 5158 to 5.40 with 22.2× speedup over 4M tokens. SnapKV (2404.14469) selects per-head with an observation window for 3.6× generation speed and 8.2× memory at 16K. PyramidKV (2406.02069) varies the budget by layer and matches a full cache at 12% retention. Scissorhands (2305.17118) reaches 5×, or 20× with 4-bit.

KV quantization. KIVI (2402.02750) uses 2-bit asymmetric quantization (per-channel keys, per-token values) for 2.6× less memory and 2.35–3.47× throughput. KVQuant (2401.18079) does pre-RoPE per-channel non-uniform quantization with a dense-and-sparse split, staying under 0.1 perplexity at 3-bit and fitting 1M-token context on one A100.

Attention architecture. MQA (1911.02150) and GQA (2305.13245) share KV heads; you can uptrain MHA to GQA at about 5% of pretraining compute with near-MHA quality. Sliding-window attention appears in Longformer (2004.05150), BigBird (2007.14062), and Mistral-7B (2310.06825). For RAG, RECOMP (2310.04408) adds extractive and abstractive summarizers with selective augmentation at 6% compression and little loss.

Chapter 5B — Measuring Cost With Model Ops in Place

Once you self-host fine-tuned SLMs, per-token API pricing stops describing your cost. Cost becomes a function of utilization and amortization:

C_request = C_marginal + C_infra_amortized + C_modelops_amortized

Marginal serving cost:

C_per_1M_tok = GPU_$per_hr / (T_tok_per_sec × 3600 × U) × 1e6

Worked example, using the throughput figures from Chapter 4B: an A100 at $3/hr, a 7B model at about 2,500 tok/s (INT4, continuous batching), U=0.7, gives about $0.48 per 1M output tokens, against $15–75 for a frontier API, a 30–150× difference. Utilization U is the largest lever, and routing raises it by concentrating traffic on the SLM, so routing saves twice.

Amortized Model-Ops cost:

C_modelops_amortized = (C_train + C_teacher_data) / N_life

A high-volume domain amortizes a $2K distillation down to about $0.0001 per request; a thin domain does not, which is a reason to use RAG rather than a bespoke adapter there. Re-distillation cadence is a cost knob, not only a quality one.

Multi-LoRA allocation (S-LoRA, Punica): the base GPU is a shared fixed cost, allocated by token share; per-domain marginal cost is roughly the SGMV overhead plus a small amortized training term. Domain N+1 adds no GPU.

Instrumentation. Use DCGM-exporter into Prometheus for live U and $/hr. Register the computed self-hosted $/1M into Langfuse, OpenLIT, or LiteLLM custom pricing so traces show real cost instead of $0. Keep an amortization ledger in the model registry. Tag every trace with domain, tier, escalated, and cache_hit. Emit OpenTelemetry GenAI semantic conventions so API and self-hosted accounting line up.

Chapter 6 — Joint Optimization

Break-even, self-host versus API: N* = Fixed / (API/req − self/req). For a distilled 7B on one A100 (about $2,160/mo) against a $0.01 API call, the crossover is roughly 240K requests/month; typical cases land in the 100K–500K range. Routine insurance volume sits above it.

Distill-then-route cost. The router sends fraction p to the self-hosted SLM (first-pass success s) and 1−p to the frontier model:

C_task = p·[ C_slm + (1−s)·C_api ] + (1−p)·C_api

The (1−s)·C_api term is the reason the two disciplines are one problem. A cheap SLM with low success is not cheap, because every failure pays for both models. The same cost is set by the L/R ratio p/(1−p·s) from 5.1.1: the remote-touch rate (1−p·s) is exactly the coefficient on C_api, so maximizing L/R at fixed quality is the same optimization as minimizing C_task. With p≈0.85, high s, and C_slm≈0.1·C_api, the blended cost is about 0.235·C_api, roughly 4×; reported real-world gains at equal task success are 5–10×. RouterBench’s finding that GPT-4 is seldom chosen is the empirical signature of this.

The loop closes through telemetry: a rising escalation rate means the SLM is drifting, which triggers the next re-distillation. Choose the cadence to minimize C_train/N_life plus the drift-induced escalation cost.

7.3 Claims and underwriting ML

Fraud detection uses graph methods over claim–policyholder–broker–garage networks. BiRank (2009.08313) shows network features beat claim-only models; AutoFraudNet (2301.07526) fuses image, text, and metadata in a cascade for +3% PR-AUC; graph-plus-gradient-boosting hybrids add SHAP interpretability. For pricing, interpretable neural additive models and tabular transformers have been benchmarked against GLM, GBT, and CANN, but transparent GLM/GAM (as in Akur8) stay preferred where regulators need explainability. Several 2026-dated agentic-orchestration papers surfaced in the scan but could not be independently verified, so they are omitted pending confirmation.

7.4 Reliability and compliance

High-stakes domains need calibration and citation grounding. Financial GraphRAG hallucination benchmarks flag that hallucinations can cause regulatory violations. Claim-level confidence calibration lets you send low-confidence outputs to human review, which is the same threshold as the routing and HITL gate in Chapter 5. For PII/PHI, Presidio (MIT; NER, regex, checksums, and DICOM image redaction) handles de-identification, though no detector is exhaustive, so layer defenses. For audit, write a Model Card (1810.03993) per adapter and checkpoint.

Chapter 8 — Multi-Domain Design

A domain (a line of business: Auto, Home, Health, Life, Commercial P&C, Workers’ Comp, Cyber) is a LoRA adapter, a RAG collection, and a config profile under one registry key. All three hot-swap on shared infrastructure (an S-LoRA base GPU, one Qdrant instance, one graph), so N domains cost about the GPUs of one base model.

domains:
  auto:
    adapter: s3://models/adapters/auto-claims-3b-v4    # LoRA on shared base
    rag_collection: auto_policies                        # Qdrant collection
    doc_schema: schemas/auto_fnol.json                   # guided-decoding target
    guardrails: [pii, auto_coverage_check]
    router: { slm_confidence_threshold: 0.82, escalation_model: llama-70b }
    keywords: [collision, vehicle, vin, at-fault]        # domain classifier
  workers_comp:
    adapter: s3://models/adapters/wc-claims-3b-v2
    rag_collection: wc_regulations
    doc_schema: schemas/wc_froi.json
    guardrails: [pii, phi, jurisdiction_check]
    router: { slm_confidence_threshold: 0.90, escalation_model: llama-70b }  # stricter: medical + legal
    keywords: [injury, employer, froi, return-to-work]

Two routers sit orthogonally. A domain classifier answers “which line of business,” using embedding similarity over the keywords or, often, the intake channel that already knows, and picks the adapter, collection, and profile. The tier router (Chapter 5) then picks SLM versus frontier at that domain’s threshold.

Onboarding a new line, say Cyber: curate data, distill a LoRA (hours, a few MB), index a Qdrant collection, write a profile, and add one registry block. vLLM hot-loads it with no redeploy.

Cost Ops runs per domain. Each line gets its own threshold τ and re-distillation cadence: strict τ for high-exposure lines (Workers’ Comp, Life, where PHI and legal exposure are high), looser τ for high-volume routine (Auto, Travel, Pet). Every trace is tagged with its domain, so cost per resolved task, escalation rate, and drift are reported per line, and a rising escalation rate in one line triggers only that line’s re-distillation.

There are two axes: line of business and workflow (underwriting, FNOL, servicing, fraud). Compose them with paired keys (auto/claims) or stacked adapters (base plus line-LoRA plus workflow-LoRA on multi-LoRA). Start with the line-of-business axis and split by workflow only when it diverges enough to earn its own adapter.

Chapter 9 — Reference Blueprint and Open Questions

9.1 End-to-end claims triage on open source

  1. Ingest: Docling, Donut, or LayoutLMv3 extract fields, with guided decoding forcing the schema.
  2. Ground: Qdrant hybrid retrieval (BGE plus bge-reranker); prefix-cache the stable guideline context; optionally compress long medical or legal documents with RECOMP or LongLLMLingua.
  3. Route: a domain classifier selects the per-domain adapter; a tier router (RouteLLM or a cascade) sends routine cases to a distilled 3–7B SLM on vLLM multi-LoRA (INT4) and escalates low-confidence or complex cases to Llama-70B or a frontier model.
  4. Guard: Presidio, Qwen3Guard, and Guardrails AI for PII/PHI, jurisdiction, and coverage checks.
  5. Human-in-the-loop: a Pydantic AI durable pause for adjuster sign-off above a value or uncertainty threshold, which is a calibrated confidence gate.
  6. Observe: Langfuse traces; report cost per resolved claim, escalation rate, SLM success s, cache hit rate, and U from DCGM.
  7. Close the loop: adjuster corrections become new training data; a rising escalation rate triggers re-distillation.

9.2 Build versus buy

Buy (Shift, Bevaya, Cytora) for speed to value when you lack a labeled corpus or an ML team; you are renting their data advantage. Build on open source when you own a large claims and policy corpus and want that advantage in-house, along with on-prem PII/PHI control and better long-run unit economics. FinGPT shows the specialization cost is small (under $300 for a LoRA); the recurring costs are data curation and serving, both governed by Chapter 5B.

9.3 Open questions

  1. Continuation routing for multi-turn claims: a POMDP-style turn-to-turn escalate/relax policy, not yet published at benchmark scale.
  2. Routing under distribution shift: how fast can a router detect a stale distilled SLM on a new claim type and rebalance τ? This ties GraphRouter’s inductivity to drift.
  3. Auditable rationale across a split decision: when an SLM and a frontier model both contribute, produce one regulator-grade explanation.
  4. Optimal re-distillation cadence: a closed form that minimizes training plus serving plus escalation cost under measured drift.
  5. Compression versus compliance: bound the risk that 20× prompt compression or aggressive KV eviction drops legally material context.

Appendix A — Insurance Primer

These concepts apply across every line of business. Architecturally they are the shared base or core-adapter layer: train them once (via DAPT or a core adapter) so per-domain adapters only encode differences.

Six principles: risk transfer and pooling; insurable interest; utmost good faith; indemnity; subrogation; contribution and proximate cause.

Parties: the insured, the carrier, the producer, the underwriter, the adjuster, the actuary, the reinsurer, the MGA, and the TPA. Agents have to model who has authority.

Two lifecycles, the same shape across lines. Policy: submission, underwriting, quote, bind, issuance (dec page), servicing and endorsements, renewal, cancellation. Claims: FNOL, triage, investigation and coverage, reserving, adjudication, settlement, subrogation, closure.

Money: premium (earned and unearned), deductible or retention or SIR, limit and sub-limit, coverage versus exclusion, coinsurance and copay, reserves (case plus IBNR). KPIs: loss ratio, expense ratio, and combined ratio (under 100% means an underwriting profit).

Underwriting: rating factors, rate/rule/form, loss cost, adverse selection, moral hazard, GLM and GAM.

Common documents: the application, ACORD forms, the dec page, policy wording, endorsements, the certificate of insurance, the loss run, the statement of value, and the FNOL/FROI.

Regulatory backbone: U.S. state regulation under the NAIC, PII/PHI rules (HIPAA, GDPR), fair and explainable decisioning, anti-fraud rules, and auditability.

Shared versus specialized: the principles, lifecycle shape, financial vocabulary, common documents, and baseline guardrails are shared; coverage triggers, jurisdiction, medical and legal nuance, and domain forms specialize.
